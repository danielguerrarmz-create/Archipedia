# Persona 04 — Marcus Feldt, Principal / Owner

> *"Two questions. Does it help us win work and make the office faster — and where
> does my client's confidential competition brief go the second someone uploads it?
> Answer those and I'll buy seats. Dodge them and I won't."*

## Snapshot

- **Age range:** 38–62 (wide on purpose — some founded young, some are veterans)
- **Role:** Founding Principal / Owner / Managing Partner of a 15–80-person practice.
- **Years in practice:** 0–30. The "0" cases are sharp young founders who started
  their own studio fast; the "30" cases are veterans who own the firm and the brand.
- **Authority:** Total — over budget, tools, hiring, brand, and what touches client
  data. He is the **adopter and the gatekeeper**, not the daily operator.
- **What he does all day:** pitching and winning work, client relationships, brand
  and reputation, business development, reviewing fees and utilization, the
  occasional high-level design steer, and saying yes/no to new software and the
  liability it carries.
- **Tools:** Less hands-on software, more Keynote/PowerPoint for pitches, the firm's
  monograph and website, a CRM, financial dashboards (utilization, realization),
  Bluebeam to mark up a fee proposal, and whatever his design leads put in front of
  him. He reads the *room* and the *contract*, not the node graph.

## Goals

1. **Win work.** Better, faster, more credible pitch materials and precedent
   narratives that help the firm beat competitors in interviews and RFPs.
2. **Protect the brand.** Everything the firm produces — including precedent boards
   — must read as rigorous and unmistakably *theirs*.
3. **Protect client data.** Confidential briefs, competition materials, and
   unbuilt-project imagery cannot leak into a third-party tool or train someone's
   model. This is contractual and reputational.
4. **Get ROI / efficiency.** If interns and intermediates spend less time hunting
   precedent and more time designing, that's billable leverage he can measure.
5. **De-risk adoption.** No tool that could embarrass the firm (fabricated
   precedent), expose client material, or fragment the team's workflow.

## Context & a day-in-the-life

He's prepping for a shortlist interview against two larger firms for a civic
commission. The win hinges on a sharp design narrative and the impression that his
boutique firm punches above its weight. His design lead (Lena) is assembling the
precedent argument; his intermediates and interns (Dev, Maya) are doing the legwork.
Separately, his ops-minded brain is always running: utilization, software spend,
and the standing fear that someone uploads a confidential client image to a free web
tool and it ends up somewhere it shouldn't. He'll evaluate Archipedia in ten
minutes, mostly by *vibe and trust signals*, then ask his design lead "is this real,
should we standardize on it?"

## How he'd use Archipedia

- Personally: skim it once, judge whether the *output* (boards, narratives) elevates
  the firm's brand and whether the *risk posture* (data, accuracy) is acceptable.
- Organizationally: if it passes, buy seats and have Lena standardize it across the
  team as the precedent-research method — turning Maya/Dev hours into leverage.
- In a pitch: a credible, credited precedent board that makes the small firm look
  rigorous and well-resourced.
- He will *not* be drawing node graphs. He judges the product by its trust surface
  and its output quality.

## Pain points & what would make him churn (or never adopt)

- **Unanswered data-security question.** If he can't quickly find what happens to
  uploaded client imagery — stored? trained on? shared? — he will not let the firm
  upload confidential material, and the tool's headline feature (image search) is
  dead on arrival for real project work.
- **Any chance of fabricated precedent.** A single invented building in a client
  pitch is a brand and credibility catastrophe. Worse than no tool.
- **Shareability ambiguity.** "Shared boards are accessible to anyone with the
  link" is exactly the sentence that makes him nervous about client work leaking.
- **No clear ROI story.** If he can't see how it saves billable hours or improves
  win rate, it's a discretionary expense he'll cut.
- **Fragmentation / a toy.** If it feels like a clever student project rather than a
  professional instrument, he won't bet the firm's process on it.

## What would delight him

- A crisp, in-context **security & confidentiality** answer at the point of upload
  and on an enterprise page — "uploads are processed in memory and discarded, not
  stored, not used to train models, not shared" — stated plainly, not buried in §1
  of a legal policy.
- **Defensible, never-fabricated** results he can trust in front of a client.
- Output that visibly **elevates the firm's brand** — exports that look like *his*
  firm, not a generic mood board.
- Clear **board ownership, autosave, and share-scope controls** (private vs.
  anyone-with-link) so client material is governed.
- A simple **ROI / efficiency** narrative he can repeat to his partners.

## Success metric

He can answer his two questions — *"does it help us win work?"* and *"is my client's
data safe?"* — affirmatively within ten minutes of looking, with enough confidence
to authorize seats and have his design lead standardize it. Downstream: measurable
reduction in precedent-hunting hours and at least one pitch where the precedent
board demonstrably helped.

## First-run review

**First 60 seconds.** The splash impresses him — it looks like a serious, designed
product, not a hackathon demo. "The index of the built world," the credited hero
precedent, the rigor of the narrative ("a sheet that holds up in review") all signal
quality. His gut says *promising*. Then the gatekeeper instincts switch on.

**Where he gets confused / hits an error.** His very first real question — *"if my
team uploads a confidential competition image, where does it go?"* — has no in-line
answer anywhere near the upload. The honest, reassuring answer *does* exist (Privacy
Policy §1: images processed in memory and discarded, not used to train, not shared),
but it's buried in a legal document he'd have to go hunting for. To a Principal, an
unanswered data question at the upload point is a *no*. Next, exploring the product
with his design lead, he learns the canvas can show results while another page
reports the index is down — and the instant he hears "those canvas results might be
mock/fake," it's over: a tool that *can* fabricate precedent is a brand liability he
will not accept. He also notices the **RUN** button does nothing visible on failure
(reads as "broken product") and that "shared boards are accessible to anyone with
the link" — a confidentiality flag for client work.

**What feels weak/static.** There's no ROI or efficiency story aimed at him — no
sense of "this turns X intern-hours into Y." The light marketing pages and the dark
canvas feel like two products, which undercuts the "one rigorous instrument" case
he'd make to his partners.

**Fixes he'd ask for (prioritized):**
1. **Answer the data question where it's asked.** A plain-language "what happens to
   your uploads" assurance at the upload point and on a security/enterprise page —
   not only in the legal policy. (→ F7)
2. **Guarantee the product can never fabricate precedent.** Kill the canvas mock
   fallback; fail honestly and identically everywhere. One fake building in a pitch
   ends us. (→ F1, F3)
3. **Govern sharing.** Explicit private vs. anyone-with-link controls, visible
   ownership and autosave, so client material is never accidentally public. (→ F10)
4. **Make RUN — the one action — behave like a finished product** (state + honest
   error), and unify the two visual languages so it reads as one instrument. (→ F2, F6)
5. **Give me the ROI line.** A simple, repeatable efficiency/brand argument I can
   take to my partners.
